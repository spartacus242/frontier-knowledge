# Daily summary, 2026-11-24

## 08:53 UTC

**Problem:** a 40-year-old conjecture about pairs of quadratic number fields. Each real quadratic field has a "mirror"
imaginary field, and a 1932 theorem of Scholz says a certain size measure (the 3-rank) of the mirror is either equal or
one larger. The conjecture (Dutarte 1984, Fouvry and Klueners 2010) says how often it is one larger: 1/3 of the time when
the rank is 0, 1/9 when it is 1, 1/27 when it is 2, about 29.77% overall. The published numerical evidence is two samples
of 1,000 fields.

**Label:** PROMISING, not verified. The skeptic gate went 1 of 3.

**Best insight:** the exact counts are now known for every one of the 3.04 billion pairs with discriminant up to 10^10,
and they are solid: two reviewers rebuilt the table to 10^9 with their own programs and one rebuilt it to 10^10, digit for
digit, with no exception to Scholz's theorem anywhere. What did not survive is the statistical reading. The raw
frequencies are still drifting at 10^10 (29.52% overall against the conjectured 29.77%), so any verdict depends on how the
drift is extrapolated. The extrapolation fixed in advance agreed with the conjecture at 10^9 but misses by 4 standard
errors for rank 1 at 10^10, and two reviewers showed the agreement at 10^9 was fragile anyway. So the data neither
confirm nor refute the conjecture; they show the approach to the limit is slower and less simple than one correction term.

**Next step:** fix the test, not the data: derive the shape of the drift from theory before fitting, or test a quantity
that has an exact prediction at finite range. One planned limb (the full group structure, not just the rank) needs the
PARI library, which this environment did not allow installing.

## 20:53 UTC

**Problem:** the same conjecture as this morning (how often a quadratic field's mirror field has a larger 3-rank), with a
better test: fix a formula on the numbers already seen, then check whether it predicts numbers not yet counted.

**Label:** PROMISING, not verified. The skeptic gate went 0 of 3.

**Best insight:** the count now covers all 9.6 billion pairs up to about 3.2 x 10^10, and two reviewers rebuilt the whole
table with their own programs, digit for digit. The formula fixed in advance (the conjectured limits plus two correction
terms) predicted the new range for all four quantities, while the simpler one-term formula failed, so the conjecture
survived a test it could have failed. But the test is modest: it pins the limits only to about 3 parts in 10,000, and the
rank-2 result fails under a stricter error estimate. All three reviewers rejected the claim, mainly because the packet
said no comparable test was published without citing Belabas's 2004 paper, which checks one slice of the same law much
further out. That omission was ours.

**Next step:** read Kraft and Washington's 2007 paper, which may already contain a joint test, before spending more on
this. The data are solid; what is left is framing, so the next run should probably move to a new problem.
