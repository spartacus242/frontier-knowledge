Question (headline): Can one convince another party that a statement is true while revealing nothing except the fact that it is true?

Question (canonical): For the membership statements 'x is in L' of an NP-complete language L (declared instance: graph 3-colourability), does there exist a two-party randomised interactive protocol in which a prover makes a probabilistic polynomial-time verifier accept every true statement and no cheating prover makes it accept a false one except with negligible probability, such that every probabilistic polynomial-time party talking to the prover on a true x can compute afterwards nothing (beyond a negligible advantage) that it could not already compute from x and the bare fact that x is in L - yes being a stated definition plus a protocol plus a proof (unconditional, or under a named intractability assumption of the kind already used for encryption), no being a proof that any language admitting such a protocol is already decidable in probabilistic polynomial time?
(No Gate 1 reformulation used; the developer's wording is carried unchanged.)

Alternative formulations:
1. Is the class of languages whose membership can be proved interactively with zero additional computational advantage to the verifier strictly larger than the languages a probabilistic polynomial-time machine decides alone, and does it contain all of NP (yes: a proved protocol for an NP-complete language; no: a collapse theorem)?
2. Given a 3-colourable graph, can a party holding a colouring convince a polynomial-time sceptic that a colouring exists, with error below any inverse polynomial, such that the sceptic's whole view gives it no help in any polynomial-time computation that the graph and the fact alone would not give - or is some such help provably unavoidable in every sound protocol?

Reason for silence: (MY OWN REASONING, not a source finding.) Three things had to be in one head at once and, on the evidence opened, were in separate papers until 1982: (1) the complexity-theoretic notion of proof was a static string, under which the question is trivially no because the verifier keeps the certificate; (2) the only rigorous secrecy notion was Shannon's, under which the question is vacuous because x already determines everything; a computational secrecy definition appears only in 1982 and only for ciphertexts (Goldwasser-Micali, opened); (3) protocol designers met the need one protocol at a time and had a working substitute - disclose everything at the end (Goldwasser-Micali step 9; Yao Section 3.3) - so nothing forced the general question. The question becomes statable only once randomised interaction is admitted as proof AND secrecy is made computational; both ingredients are months old at the as-of date.
