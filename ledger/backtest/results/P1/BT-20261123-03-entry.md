Question (headline): Can a single atomic layer of graphite exist as a stable crystal on its own, and do its electrons behave as massless relativistic particles?
Question (canonical): If a sheet of sp2-bonded carbon exactly one atom thick is separated from bulk graphite so that it is neither chemically bonded nor epitaxially registered to a substrate (arm A: suspended across an aperture; arm B: resting on a chemically inert insulator), does a layer-count-resolved structural probe show it keeping long-range hexagonal crystalline order at micrometre lateral scale at room temperature for days or longer, and do its charge carriers show a linear energy-momentum relation with zero rest mass - cyclotron mass vanishing as the square root of carrier density, Landau-level energies scaling as the square root of magnetic field with a field-independent level at zero energy, and a half-period phase shift of magneto-oscillations and Hall plateaus - where YES is both, and NO is either that no such sheet persists or that the carriers show a finite density-independent mass, a gap at the band-touching point, or the conventional phase and plateau sequence?
Reformulation note: none used at Gate 1; intake wording carried unchanged.
Alternative formulations: (1) Is the thinning limit of graphite reachable - one carbon layer held by van der Waals contact or nothing, identified as exactly one layer, diffracting as a hexagonal crystal at 300 K, with cyclotron mass, Landau spectrum and oscillation phase of the massless two-dimensional Dirac form rather than bulk graphite's massive form? (2) Does an unsupported or inertly supported graphite monolayer have (i) a finite, time-stable crystalline order parameter at room temperature and (ii) gapless quasiparticles with E = v|p|, each half reported separately?
