## Question (headline)

*Carried verbatim from the ranker:*

> Can blocking an inhibitory receptor on T cells release an immune response strong enough to reject established tumours?

## Question (canonical — operational test, version 1 as submitted)

In immunocompetent syngeneic mice already bearing palpable, established tumours of a weakly immunogenic transplantable line, does systemic administration of a monoclonal antibody that blocks the T-cell inhibitory receptor CTLA-4 (begun only after tumours are measurable, with an isotype-matched antibody as control) cause complete and durable rejection in a substantial fraction of animals — yes if a significant share of antibody-treated hosts clear established tumour and stay tumour-free, with rejection abolished by depletion of T cells; no if blockade leaves growth curves and tumour-free survival indistinguishable from control despite confirmed receptor occupancy and evidence of enhanced T-cell responsiveness elsewhere in the same animals?

## Question (canonical — version 2, the one permitted Gate 1 reformulation; well-posedness only)

In immunocompetent syngeneic mice bearing palpable established tumours of **≥5 mm mean diameter** of a **named** weakly immunogenic transplantable line — the line declared before any treatment and qualified in a pre-run control arm of the same colony as showing **≤10% spontaneous rejection and ≤10% rejection after immunisation with irradiated cells of that line** — does systemic anti-CTLA-4 monoclonal antibody, begun only after that size is reached, versus an isotype-matched control antibody, produce **complete regression with no palpable tumour for ≥60 days in ≥30% of treated hosts** against ≤5% in control, with the effect **abolished by combined CD4/CD8 depletion** and with surviving hosts **resisting rechallenge** with the same line, under **documented receptor occupancy** on T cells including draining lymphoid tissue?

Both versions answer the same headline. Version 2 exists only to repair one defect in version 1: version 1 refers to a "substantial fraction", a "pre-declared durable interval" and a "weakly immunogenic transplantable line" as if pre-declared and enumerates none of the three — the well-posedness defect of a pre-declared set that is never listed nor generated by a stated rule. Version 2 states the rule (the ≤10%/≤10% qualification criterion) rather than listing lines, and fixes the two numbers.

**What the repair itself presupposes** (tested as hard as the clause it replaced):
1. A line meeting the ≤10%/≤10% criterion exists in the colony used — **UNVERIFIED**; it must be shown by the pre-run arm, not assumed. If no line qualifies, version 2 has no admissible subject and version 1 must be used with its vagueness flagged.
2. A 60-day tumour-free interval in that line is distinguishable from slow regrowth in its natural history — **UNVERIFIED** for an unnamed line on pre-1996 print.
3. ≥30% is a design choice, not a prediction. The PARTIAL clause stays intact: 10–29% complete regression is PARTIAL (blockade modulates), not a NO, and the repair must not be read as converting a Partial into a failure.
4. Depletion antibodies deplete effectively in a tumour-bearing host — standard pre-1996 premise, required to be demonstrated in the same animals.

## Alternatives

- Does antibody interruption of a negative signalling receptor on T cells, given after tumour establishment rather than as a prophylaxis, convert progressive growth of a poorly immunogenic syngeneic tumour into T-cell-dependent complete regression?
- Is the ceiling on anti-tumour T-cell responses in a tumour-bearing host set by an inhibitory receptor on the T cell itself — testable by whether blocking that receptor alone, with no vaccine, cytokine or adoptive transfer, suffices to eradicate a tumour that is already growing?

## Domain(s)

## Motivating gap

As of 1996-01-01, CTLA-4 had been reported as a negative rather than a second positive regulator of T-cell activation, and the brake had been shown to be load-bearing in vivo by deletion. Tumour immunology of the period located the failure of anti-tumour immunity on the tumour side or in insufficient costimulation, and tried to fix it by **adding** signal — costimulatory-ligand transfection of tumour cells, cytokine gene transfer, vaccines, adoptive transfer. The untested inversion is subtraction: remove a brake on the host's T cells, in a host whose tumour is already established, with nothing added.

Read levels for the gap's own anchors: **Krummel & Allison 1995 — FULL TEXT, opened** (see Prior 1). **Walunas et al., Immunity 1994;1(5):793–801; Waterhouse et al., Science 1995;270(5238):985–988; Tivol et al., Immunity 1995;3(5):541–547 — NOT OPENED in this run** (cited from the intake draft, which itself cited them from memory). They are pre-as-of and admissible in principle, but as unopened items here they ground no grade in either direction, and the gap statement above is written so that it does not depend on them beyond the direction of effect that Prior 1's own abstract establishes.

## What would count as an answer

**YES:** pre-declared design, treatment begun only after tumours reach the declared size; ≥30% of blockade-treated hosts complete regression and tumour-free ≥60 days against ≤5% in isotype control; growth curves separate; effect abolished by T-cell depletion; survivors resist rechallenge (memory, which distinguishes immune rejection from a direct drug effect on tumour cells).
**NO:** with documented receptor occupancy and a positive internal control showing the antibody does amplify T-cell responses in the same animals, growth and tumour-free survival statistically indistinguishable from control.
**PARTIAL, reported as such:** growth delay without clearance; or clearance only in small tumours or only with a co-administered vaccine or cytokine — that answers the weaker "blockade modulates" question and leaves the headline's "strong enough to reject established tumours" open.
**UNINTERPRETABLE:** death from autoimmunity or antibody toxicity before the tumour endpoint.

The answer is the result of an experiment on animals and their tumours. No literature survey, record audit or protocol comparison could substitute, and none is part of the test.

## Gate 3

**Consequence (argued, not asserted).** YES changes the field's causal model: the deficit becomes inhibition rather than insufficient stimulation, and the therapeutic target moves from the tumour to the host's own T cell — no tumour antigen need be identified, no vaccine or cytokine supplied. That is a different kind of intervention from everything the period was building (Prior 2's three strategies, Prior 5's transfectants), and it predicts its own toxicity (autoimmunity) as a mechanistic consequence rather than an accident. NO is equally consequential and is the reason the question is worth asking rather than assuming: it would locate the failure of tumour immunity upstream of inhibitory signalling and confine the negative-regulator account of CTLA-4 to homeostasis of normal responses — and it would do so in the one setting where the costimulator-versus-inhibitor dispute (presupposition 1) has clinical stakes.

**Audience:** tumour immunologists, T-cell immunologists, transplantation and autoimmunity researchers; and, on a YES, clinical oncology.

**Fecundity:** a YES generates an immediate programme — other inhibitory receptors, dose and timing relative to establishment, combination with the period's supply-side strategies, the autoimmune cost of brake removal, and whether the same brake can be *engaged* for transplantation tolerance. A NO generates the complementary programme of locating the limiting step.

**Anchoring:** anchored to one opened full text (Prior 1) for the receptor's direction of effect and the admitted uncertainty about its function; the design's own internal controls (occupancy, isotype, depletion, rechallenge) anchor the answer rather than any literature.

**Gate 3 score: 3/3.**

**Reason for silence (candidate mechanism).** Three things pointed the period's effort away from this experiment. The causal model put the fault on the tumour side (weak immunogenicity, antigen or MHC loss) or in missing costimulation, so the research instinct was to **add** signal, which is what Prior 2's abstract enumerates and Prior 5 exemplifies. CTLA-4's function was unsettled — it had been reported as a CD28-like costimulator, and Prior 1 says in its own words that less is known about its function — so a blockade therapy would have been a bet on the newer of two live readings. And the deletion phenotype framed systemic brake removal as a **toxicity** experiment rather than a therapy experiment, in a tumour model the field regarded as immunologically close to invisible. Blocking reagents also existed in only a few laboratories and had been used in culture and on non-tumour in-vivo endpoints. Nothing forbade the experiment; the framing made it look like the wrong bet.
