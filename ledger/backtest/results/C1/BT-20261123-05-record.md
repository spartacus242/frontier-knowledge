Question (headline): Can one convince another party that a statement is true while revealing nothing except the fact that it is true?

Question (canonical): For a language L in NP not known to be decidable in probabilistic polynomial time (declared test members: quadratic residuosity modulo a composite of unknown factorisation, and Boolean satisfiability), does there exist a two-party randomised interactive protocol in which a prover makes a probabilistic polynomial-time verifier accept every true statement 'x is in L', no prover strategy makes it accept a false one except with negligible probability, and everything the verifier can efficiently compute after the interaction it could have computed efficiently from x alone given that x is in L - yes if such a protocol is proved for at least one declared language (unconditionally or under a stated intractability assumption), no if it is proved that any language with such a protocol is decidable in probabilistic polynomial time.
(No Gate 1 reformulation used; this is the supplied wording unchanged.)

Alternative formulations: (1) Is there a language outside probabilistic polynomial time (or not known to be inside it) whose membership can be demonstrated by an interactive, coin-tossing exchange that is complete, sound up to negligible error, and whose entire verifier-side view can be reproduced by an efficient algorithm given only the true statement - or does every sound demonstration of membership necessarily hand the verifier some computation it could not have done alone? (2) Take the standard NP proof of 'x is in L' (send the witness): can the witness-holder replace it with a randomised conversation after which a polynomially bounded verifier is convinced, yet is no better able than before to compute any function of the witness or to convince a third party - shown by a protocol with proofs of completeness, soundness and non-disclosure for quadratic residuosity or satisfiability, or refuted by a theorem that sound conviction on a hard language always transfers extractable computational advantage?

Significance - consequence: Yes: a proof need not be a transferable object; authentication and protocol-correctness steps that in 1982 are done by revealing secrets afterwards (A: factorisations released at the end of the game; C: "force both parties to reveal their variables") could be done during the protocol without disclosure. No: a lower bound tying soundness to leakage, i.e. every verifiable claim costs secret material, which bounds what the 1981-82 protocol programme (coin flipping, mental poker, secret exchange, secure computation) can achieve against cheaters. Either answer is a theorem about a class of computations.

Reason for silence: A new conceptual bridge that is months old at the as-of date, plus a disciplinary boundary. The bridge: a computational (rather than Shannon) meaning of "no information" appears in print only in 1982 (A sec. 2.5; C sec. 3.1), and only for ciphertexts and function evaluation; before it, "a proof that reveals nothing" has no statable meaning, since any transcript correlated with a witness carries Shannon information.
