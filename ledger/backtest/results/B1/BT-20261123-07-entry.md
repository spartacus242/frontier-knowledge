Question (headline): Do the clustered regularly interspaced repeats found in many bacterial and archaeal genomes defend the cell against foreign DNA such as viruses?
Question (canonical): In one or more of four enumerated hosts (Escherichia coli K-12, Streptococcus thermophilus, Haloferax volcanii, Sulfolobus solfataricus), does an isogenic trio — (a) wild type, (b) wild type with one spacer of the repeat array replaced by 30–40 nt taken from a phage or plasmid the host is demonstrably permissive to, (c) strain (b) additionally deleted for the adjacent cas genes — show the ordering (a) ≈ (c) >> (b) in that invader's efficiency of plaquing or plasmid transformation/retention, a reduction of >= 1 order of magnitude that is abolished by cas deletion and absent for a sequence-unmatched control invader; with the secondary limb, runnable only where some resident spacer matches an obtainable invader, being whole-array and cas deletion challenged with that invader?
Reformulation note: this is VERSION 2, the one permitted Gate 1 repair of the submitted version 1 (whole-array deletion against a resident-spacer-matched invader). The repair was made because P3 — that some resident spacer matches an obtainable invader — is unestablished pre-as-of. The repair introduces two presuppositions of its own (R1 array editability, R2 one-spacer sufficiency), both UNVERIFIED; see Presuppositions.
Alternative formulations: (1) Is the infectivity of a phage or plasmid on a prokaryotic host a function of sequence identity between the invader and the host's interspaced-repeat spacers, such that removing the array or the cas genes abolishes that dependence? (2) Do phage and plasmid establishment frequencies track array spacer content in a cas-dependent, sequence-specific way, or are they indifferent to it?
