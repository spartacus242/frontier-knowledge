## 1. Restatement in my own words (two layers)

**Headline, as I read it.** Those chromosomal stretches of short identical repeats separated by variable
one-off spacers, which turn up in a large share of bacterial and most archaeal genomes and currently have
no assigned job, are they a defence system — do they stop incoming foreign DNA (phage, plasmid) from
establishing in the cell?

**Operational test, as it now stands (version 2, the one permitted Gate 1 reformulation — I read the
question repair included, per the 2026-10-22 18:33 rule).** Take at least one of four named hosts
(*E. coli* K-12, *S. thermophilus*, *Haloferax volcanii*, *Sulfolobus solfataricus*). Build an isogenic
trio: (a) wild type; (b) the same strain with ONE spacer in the repeat array swapped for 30-40 nt lifted
from a phage or plasmid that strain is demonstrably permissive to; (c) strain (b) with the adjacent *cas*
genes deleted. Primary limb: does the swapped spacer cut that invader's efficiency of plaquing (or plasmid
transformation/retention) by >=10x versus (a), with the cut abolished in (c) and absent for a
sequence-unmatched control invader — i.e. the ordering (a) ~ (c) >> (b) on the matched invader only?
Secondary limb, runnable only where some resident spacer happens to match an obtainable invader: delete the
whole array or the *cas* genes and see whether that invader's establishment rises >=10x.
Headline answer rides on the primary limb; the stated NO branch rides on both.
