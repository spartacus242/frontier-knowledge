## Restatement, in my own words

**Headline layer (as I read it):** some bacteria and archaea carry a chromosomal run of short identical repeats separated by short non-repeating stretches. Nobody knows what the locus does. Is what it does *fight off incoming DNA* — phage, plasmid?

**Operational layer, VERSION 2 (the live wording, Gate 1's one permitted repair):** take one tractable host from a closed list of four (*E. coli* K-12, *S. thermophilus*, *Haloferax volcanii*, *Sulfolobus solfataricus*). Build three strains differing in nothing else: (a) wild type; (b) wild type with one spacer in the array swapped for 30–40 nt lifted out of a phage or plasmid that this host is known to be *permissive* to; (c) strain (b) with the neighbouring *cas* genes deleted. Plate that phage (or transform that plasmid) on all three, plus a second invader whose sequence matches nothing. YES = the engineered strain (b) drops the matched invader's establishment by ≥ 1 log versus (a), the drop disappears in (c), and the unmatched invader is untouched — i.e. the ordering (a) ≈ (c) ≫ (b). NO = everything within error, across ≥ 2 invaders, in strains proved sensitive and competent. A secondary limb (version 1's original) deletes the whole array or the *cas* genes and challenges with an invader matching a *resident* spacer; it is runnable only where some resident spacer happens to match an obtainable invader.

So: the question is a causal, in-cells, counted-plaques question about what a chromosomal locus does to DNA that gets inside. The headline asks the function; the operational test asks for a fold change with a pre-declared threshold.
