## Restatement, in my own words

**Headline layer (plain):** do the interspaced repeat arrays sitting in a lot of bacterial and archaeal chromosomes exist in order to stop incoming DNA — phage genomes, plasmids — from establishing in the cell?

**Operational layer (the canonical test, as it now stands after Gate 1's one repair, VERSION 2):** take one of four named hosts (E. coli K-12, S. thermophilus, Haloferax volcanii, Sulfolobus solfataricus); build three strains that differ only in the repeat locus — wild type; a strain in which one spacer of the array has been swapped for 30–40 nt lifted out of a phage or plasmid the host normally accepts; and that same engineered strain with the neighbouring cas genes deleted. Plate the matched invader and an unmatched control invader on all three. YES = the engineered spacer cuts the matched invader's plaquing or transformation frequency by at least ten-fold, the cut disappears when cas is deleted, and the unmatched invader is untouched (ordering wild type ≈ cas-deleted >> engineered). NO = everything within error across the trio on at least two invaders, in strains independently shown to be phage-sensitive and competent. A secondary limb — delete the whole array or cas and challenge with an invader that matches a *resident* spacer — is runnable only where some resident spacer happens to match an obtainable invader.

So: the headline asks what the locus is FOR; the operational test asks whether a deliberately installed piece of invader sequence, plus its neighbouring genes, changes how much of that invader gets in.
