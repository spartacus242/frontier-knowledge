Domain(s): Medicine - gastroenterology; medical microbiology; infectious cause of chronic disease

Question (headline): Are chronic gastritis and peptic ulcer disease caused by a bacterial infection of the stomach?

Question (canonical): In adults having upper endoscopy (excluding regular aspirin/anti-inflammatory users and gastrin-secreting tumour), are bacteria on or in the gastric mucosa (i) present, on blinded histology and culture of antral and body biopsies, in a clearly larger fraction of patients with histological chronic gastritis, duodenal ulcer or gastric ulcer than of patients with histologically normal mucosa, (ii) able in pure culture to produce histological gastritis when given to a host with previously normal mucosa, and (iii) such that randomised antibacterial treatment which clears them on repeat biopsy resolves the gastritis and lowers 12-month endoscopic ulcer relapse compared with acid-lowering treatment alone - YES if all three hold, NO if blinded biopsies show no association or clearance changes neither gastritis nor relapse, and COLONISER-ONLY if (i) holds while (ii) and (iii) fail?

Alternative formulations:
1. Do the three classical lines of causal evidence - consistent presence in diseased but not normal human gastric mucosa, reproduction of gastritis by a pure culture in a previously normal stomach, and cure of gastritis with a fall in ulcer recurrence after the organisms are eliminated - all hold for a bacterium living in the human stomach?
2. If the bacteria that some pathologists have seen on inflamed gastric mucosa are removed from ulcer patients by antibacterial drugs in a randomised comparison with acid suppression alone, does the gastritis heal and does the ulcer stay healed at one year, and does a pure culture of those bacteria inflame a normal stomach?

What would count as an answer: three pre-declared limbs, each reported separately for chronic gastritis, duodenal ulcer and gastric ulcer. (i) Association: consecutive endoscopy patients, antral and body biopsies, read blind to diagnosis by two pathologists using a routine stain plus at least one bacterial stain (Gram and a silver method, declared in advance - note Freedberg and Barron 1940 name the DaFano silver impregnation as a method that "will demonstrate spirochetes in the gastric tissue of the dog and human"), cultured aerobically, anaerobically and at reduced oxygen; outcome is the fraction with mucosal bacteria per diagnostic group versus histologically normal controls. YES-direction: organisms in a large majority of diseased and a small minority of normal stomachs, the same morphological type throughout. NO: similar fractions, or absence under adequate staining of fresh biopsies. (ii) Transmission: a pure culture given to a host with biopsy-proven normal mucosa, with repeat biopsy; yes if gastritis appears with the organism recoverable, no if colonisation occurs without inflammation or repeated challenge fails. (iii) Elimination: patients with organisms and endoscopically proven ulcer randomised to an acid-lowering drug alone or with a clearing regimen, blinded endoscopy and biopsy at healing and 12 months, analysed by allocation and by achieved clearance; yes if gastritis resolves and relapse is clearly lower where organisms were cleared, no if neither changes despite documented clearance. Overall YES needs (i) and (iii) with (ii) or a documented reason it cannot be done; overall NO is failure of (i) under adequate method or of (iii) despite clearance; (i) alone is COLONISER-ONLY. Not a thesis: each limb has a stated result returning no.

Reason for silence: a disciplinary boundary plus a discredited-hypothesis taboo. The bacterial hypothesis was pursued and left unproven by the 1915-1916 workers, who could show presence but not causation; one large negative survey (Palmer 1954) is widely treated as having closed it; and the acid-pepsin model supplied both a mechanism and, from the late 1970s, an effective symptomatic drug, so nobody needed another cause. The organisms kept being seen, but by histologists and electron microscopists whose papers were about epithelium and drug response, not by microbiologists, and the two 1975 papers I opened each record the observation inside a study aimed at something else. A pathologist seeing organisms had no culture; a microbiologist had no reason to culture a compartment taught to be sterile.
